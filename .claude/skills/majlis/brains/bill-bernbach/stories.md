# Stories: Bill Bernbach

16 stories and case studies he and his agency are known by, most-told first. Credit for the famous lines often belongs to DDB teams working under him; that is noted where the sources say so.

1. **The 1947 letter to Grey.** As creative director at Grey, aged 35, he warned the owners that growth would make the agency "worship techniques instead of substance" and hire routinised men with a formula. Largely ignored, he left two years later to found Doyle Dane Bernbach (1949). Lesson: technique without ideas produces sameness; a creative culture must be defended as it grows. (S001, S002, S010, S016, S019, S036, S039)

2. **Think Small (Volkswagen, 1959).** A small, odd German car from a Nazi-era plant sold to Americans who loved big chrome. Helmut Krone and Julian Koenig, under Bernbach, put a tiny car in a sea of white space, black and white, with frank copy. Ad Age named it the No. 1 campaign of the century in 1999. Lesson: embrace the product's truth and break the category look on purpose. (S020, S021, S022, S010, S027, S019)

3. **Lemon (Volkswagen, 1960).** One word over a perfect-looking car; the copy explains an inspector rejected it for a blemished glove-compartment strip. Bernbach saw the Wolfsburg inspector check cars with a magnifying glass. Lesson: an admitted flaw proves a strength; a fresh word beats a superlative. (S003, S021, S007, S020)

4. **We Try Harder (Avis, 1962).** Avis had lost money for years and was No. 2 to Hertz. The line was found in Helmut Krone's wastebasket, per Willens; the campaign tested badly, and Bernbach ran it because the half who liked it were the half he wanted. Avis moved into profit within a year. Lesson: turn your weakness into the promise; do not kill polarising work on averages. (S007, S010, S027, S048, S019)

5. **The cigarette accounts.** After the 1964 Surgeon General's report, DDB stopped handling cigarette advertising, the most profitable kind. In 1971 he said a principle is not a principle until it costs you money, and that they were "never on a crusade". After his death the agency took a cigarette account. Lesson: principles are proven by their cost, and they live in people, not in companies. (S004, S005, S046)

6. **Levy's real Jewish rye (1961 onward).** To sell a Brooklyn Jewish bread to all New Yorkers, DDB put non-Jewish faces (a choirboy, a policeman, a Penobscot railroad engineer) on subway posters: "You don't have to be Jewish to love Levy's". Line by Judy Protas; art by Bill Taubin; photos by Howard Zieff. It made Levy's New York's top-selling rye. Lesson: warm, specific humour and real faces can widen a market without changing the product. (S030, S031, S032, S033, S039)

7. **Ohrbach's (from 1949).** DDB's first client, a cheap store in an unfashionable area, was turned into "high fashion at low prices" with witty headline and picture pairs, including a snobbish cat envying her neighbour's clothes. Ohrbach paid in advance so the new agency could pay its bills. Lesson: personality can make cheap chic; Ohrbach's work later attracted Volkswagen. (S007, S026, S033)

8. **Clairol Nice 'n Easy: "It Lets Me Be Me".** Every hair-colour brand had the same research: women want to look natural. DDB kept the benefit and changed the voice to a girl saying the line; with no other change, sales rose for seven years until they doubled. Lesson: execution becomes content. (S003)

9. **Alka-Seltzer "Mamma mia, that's a spicy meatball" (1969).** An actor keeps flubbing a take for a pasta sauce ad; only at the end is the product revealed as the cure for his indigestion. Associated with Bernbach, Roy Grace and Evan Stark. Lesson: a story can earn the product reveal; humour works when the product is the punchline. (S010, S042, S008, S033)

10. **Polaroid.** From "Visit to Grandpa" with Laurence Olivier to a Louis Armstrong close-up so strong it needed no headline, DDB cut words and enlarged the picture to show "modern magic" in everyone's hands. Lesson: let the product's moment be the hero. (S008, S026, S019)

11. **The placebo and the double helix (1980 speech).** He opened his 4A's talk with a woman reacting to a sugar pill, and with Watson's "too pretty not to be it". Lesson: belief and taste shape reality; facts alone do not persuade. (S003)

12. **The father boasting about his daughter.** Brands that call their product the best in the world are like a proud father; people smile and do not believe him. Lesson: superlatives are discounted; specific truth is believed. (S003)

13. **Gablinger's beer (1967).** An excellent campaign got hundreds of thousands of New Yorkers to try a low-carb beer; for most, one can was enough. Lesson: great advertising makes a bad product fail faster. (S006)

14. **The writer and art director in one room.** At Weintraub he met Paul Rand and saw copy and art work in harmony; at Grey he paired Phyllis Robinson with Bob Gage; at DDB the pair became the standard model for agencies. Lesson: the idea lives where words and picture are conceived together. (S008, S026, S019, S022)

15. **Daisy (1964).** DDB's one political campaign, for Lyndon Johnson, produced the "Daisy" spot, aired once. It made DDB the reputed godfather of emotional political attack ads; the agency later banned political work. Lesson: persuasion skill is morally neutral; choose what you sell. (S005)

16. **Mary Wells and the "religious order".** Mary Wells Lawrence describes DDB as the place everyone was dying to work, where Bernbach "made a serious woman out of me", and where the agency enjoyed looking plain because the work was the decoration. Lesson: a creative culture is built on taste and standards, not decor. (S034)
