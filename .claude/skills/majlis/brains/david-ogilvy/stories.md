# Stories: David Ogilvy

18 stories and case studies he reuses, most-told first. Figures are as he or the source tells them; they vary between tellings.

1. **The man in the Hathaway shirt (1951).** A 106-year-old shirtmaker with a $30,000 budget promised never to change a word or fire the agency. Ogilvy had read research that photographs with "story appeal" draw more readers, wrote 18 ideas, and the last was an eye patch bought on the way to the shoot. The ad ran for decades and rescued the agency from obscurity. Lesson: research can lead you to the idea; one odd, relevant detail makes people read the copy. (S007, S008, S009, S029, S031, S035)

2. **"At 60 miles an hour the loudest noise in this new Rolls-Royce comes from the electric clock" (1958).** He knew nothing about cars, read for three weeks, and lifted the line from a motoring magazine's test report. The first ad ran 719 words of facts, the next 1,400; he says it doubled sales, while the engineers wanted to fix the noisy clock. Lesson: the best headline is often a fact you found, not a line you invented. (S008, S009, S011, S021, S007, S023)

3. **Dove is one-quarter cleansing cream (1957).** Lever wanted to sell a "neutral" bar; housewives were cold to it. A chemist's list of ingredients produced the fact (cold-cream ingredient) and the position: a toilet bar for women with dry skin, not a detergent bar for dirty hands. It ran essentially unchanged for over 25 years. Lesson: positioning comes from a product fact matched to a buyer's problem. (S007, S008, S019, S020)

4. **Commander Whitehead, the man from Schweppes.** The real head of Schweppes USA, bearded ex-Navy officer, became the face of every ad; the campaign ran about 18 to 20 years. His briefcase, which looked like it held the secret formula, held his bathing suit. Lesson: a real person from the company can be a better symbol than a hired face. (S007, S008, S032, S023)

5. **Puerto Rico and the empty room.** To change the image of a poor island, he ran editorial-style ads in the New Yorker; the Casals Festival ad showed an empty room with a cello leaning on a chair instead of the cellist. He called one Puerto Rico ad his most effective ever and was proud it helped make the island "a little less poverty stricken". Lesson: story appeal and dignity can reposition a whole country. (S002, S007, S008, S014, S033)

6. **The Aga cooker manual (1935).** As a door-to-door stove salesman in Scotland he became the top seller and wrote "The Theory and Practice of Selling the Aga Cooker", later called by Fortune probably the best sales manual ever written. It got him his first agency job. Lesson: advertising is salesmanship; know your prospect before you knock. (S018, S023, S025, S031)

7. **Gallup at Princeton.** With $10 left he got a job with George Gallup researching the film industry and learned to predict audiences before a picture was made. He called it the greatest break of his life and built his agency's reputation on research. Lesson: start your career where you learn how buyers really behave. (S007, S008, S011, S024, S025)

8. **Eleanor Roosevelt sells margarine.** He paid her $35,000 for a margarine commercial. People remembered Mrs Roosevelt and forgot the brand, and both sides of her mail were unhappy. Lesson: celebrities sell themselves, not your product. (S007, S009, S017)

9. **The Guinness guide to oysters.** Billed as research director, he had his first big idea on a train after reading a biologist's book on shellfish; it became a long series of Guinness guides. Lesson: homework plus a quiet moment produces the idea; anyone can have one. (S007, S008)

10. **Pepperidge Farm's horse and wagon.** Stuck for an idea, he went to sleep and dreamed of an old baker driving two white horses; the wagon was still delivering bread about 25 years later. Lesson: feed the unconscious, then let it work. (S007, S008, S009)

11. **Shell: one medium, all ingredients.** Shell asked which medium to drop; its own staff said newspapers. He recommended newspapers only, with big ads explaining the ingredients in gasoline, and says it arrested a long decline in share. Lesson: tell people how the product works; concentrate where you can be heard. (S007, S008)

12. **Rinso and his own blood.** For a stain-removal ad he pricked his finger and put real blood on the page. Helpful how-to copy outdrew product-only copy. Lesson: demonstrate, and give useful information. (S007, S015, S028)

13. **Direct mail grew the agency.** He took a correspondence course in direct mail at 25; at Ogilvy & Mather he mailed new-business prospects every four weeks and was amazed how many clients came from it. Lesson: measure, mail, repeat. (S006, S022)

14. **The Russian dolls.** Each new office head got a matrioshka with a note inside: hire people smaller than you and we become dwarfs, bigger and we become giants. Lesson: talent sets the ceiling of the work. (S001, S007, S008, S017)

15. **Thom McAn: testimonial over "Amazing Offer" (1955).** The agency knew the offer ad would sell more shoes short term but chose the testimonial campaign because it built the brand image. Lesson: do not trade the brand for one month of volume. (S002)

16. **Helena Rubinstein.** She fired her agency every year; he kept her for 15. Large newspaper ads that looked like news reportedly brought in a year's worth of orders in three weeks. Lesson: news-style ads with substance can outsell glamour. (S035)

17. **The nude on the cooker.** His first ad, as a young man, used Manet's painting with a naked model to sell cooking stoves; he later said he was "playing the fool". Lesson: attention that has nothing to do with the product is wasted. (S009)

18. **His mistakes.** Turning down a small office machine account called Xerox, being too soft about firing non-performers, leaving creative work for management, and weeping in front of the client when he lost Guinness. Lesson: admit mistakes early; keep doing the work you are best at. (S001, S007)
