# Frameworks: Seth Godin

9 frameworks, grouped by the job they do (who, what, how it spreads, how you talk, what you charge). "Sources" lists the sources in this brain that teach the framework. Book tags show where the idea lives in his books, as known from his blog and talks; no book text is reproduced.

---

## Who

### 1. Smallest viable audience (SVA) *(This Is Marketing)*
- **What it is:** Pick the smallest group of people that can sustain the work and that you can serve brilliantly, instead of chasing the average of everyone.
- **Parts:** (1) Who's it for and what's it for. (2) The group is small enough to delight and big enough to sustain you. (3) Delight them so deeply that they spread the word to like-minded peers. (4) Growth comes from that spread; you "discover it’s a lot larger group than you expected" (S003).
- **When to use:** Choosing a hero product's audience, writing a landing page, choosing what to say in an ad, deciding whether to launch a second product for a different crowd.
- **How to score:** Can the merchant name the group in one sentence (not "women 18 to 45")? Would that group miss the store if it closed? Does the option make the product better for them, or more average for everyone? An option that broadens the message to please everyone scores low.
- **Quote:** "Stake out the smallest market you can imagine. The smallest market that can sustain you, the smallest market you can adequately serve." (S002)
- **Sources:** S001, S002, S003, S004, S005, S017

### 2. Aim narrow to get big *(blog, 2025)*
- **What it is:** His answer to "but we want scale": market share is not reached by aiming wide and missing; it is reached by winning a tiny slice so well that it spreads.
- **Parts:** Aiming at everyone forces you to sand off the edges and become average. Embracing the 1% builds traction and word of mouth that carries you to 3%.
- **When to use:** When a merchant argues for broad targeting "because we need volume".
- **How to score:** Does the option have a believable path from the first devoted group to the next one? Or is it a 40% plan that hopes for 3%?
- **Quote:** "The thing is, you don’t get to 3% of the market by trying for 40% and failing." (S004)
- **Sources:** S004, S003

## What

### 3. Purple Cow: remarkable or invisible *(Purple Cow)*
- **What it is:** People ignore the ordinary. Remarkable means worth making a remark about, judged by the customer, not by the maker. Safe, average, "very good" products are invisible.
- **Parts:** Find the people who care obsessively (he calls it "otaku"), make something they will talk about, let it spread from them to the rest of the curve. Design is a tool for being remarkable.
- **When to use:** Product, packaging, unboxing, offer, and launch decisions. Any time the plan is "a better version of what competitors do".
- **How to score:** Would a real customer describe this to a friend unprompted? If the honest answer is "it is good", score it low. Remarkable to the target, not to the team.
- **Quote:** "The safe thing to do now is to be at the fringes, be remarkable. And being very good is one of the worst things you can possibly do." (S015)
- **Sources:** S015, S001, S013

### 4. Be the one they would miss *(blog)*
- **What it is:** The obligation that comes with a narrow audience: become the kind of provider people would miss if you were gone.
- **Parts:** No waiting for "the next random person to find us"; earn connection; set a standard for work that changes people.
- **When to use:** Retention, service, and repeat-purchase questions; deciding whether to cut service to fund ads.
- **How to score:** After this option, would the core customers notice and complain if the store disappeared?
- **Quote:** "Not everyone, but someone." (S001)
- **Sources:** S001, S013

## How it spreads and how you talk

### 5. Permission marketing *(Permission Marketing, 1999)*
- **What it is:** The privilege, not the right, of delivering anticipated, personal, and relevant messages to people who want them. Attention is something people pay you; you cannot take it back once wasted.
- **Parts:** Anticipated (they expect it), personal (to them), relevant (about what they care about). You make a promise to get permission and then you keep exactly that promise. Real permission: if you stop, people ask where you went. Presumed or legal-fine-print permission is not permission.
- **When to use:** WhatsApp and SMS broadcasts to past buyers, email flows, retargeting frequency, list building, any "let's blast the list" plan.
- **How to score:** Would recipients be upset if this message stopped coming? Is it what they signed up for? Does sending it spend trust faster than it earns?
- **Quote:** "Permission marketing is the privilege (not the right) of delivering anticipated, personal and relevant messages to people who actually want to get them." (S006)
- **Sources:** S006

### 6. Ideas that spread vs the TV-industrial complex *(TED2003, Purple Cow)*
- **What it is:** The old model bought ads to interrupt people, used the reach to get distribution, sold more, and bought more ads. That loop broke because people learned to ignore interruptions.
- **Parts:** Stop interrupting the people who ignore you; talk to the ones who care, and let them tell the others.
- **When to use:** When the plan is "more budget into interruption ads" for an undifferentiated product.
- **How to score:** Is the money buying interruptions, or building something the early, obsessed customers will pass on?
- **Quote:** "What marketers used to do is make average products for average people." (S015)
- **Sources:** S015, S009

### 7. Direct marketing vs brand marketing *(blog; This Is Marketing)*
- **What it is:** Two different games. Direct marketing is measured and must pay for itself. Brand marketing builds trust and a feeling that makes people pay extra, and it cannot be measured the same way.
- **Parts:** Measure direct ads hard and repeat what works. Run brand work patiently and consistently, in media that fit the brand. Do not let direct-response tactics become a shortcut that erodes the brand you want.
- **When to use:** Budget split between brand and performance, judging an awareness campaign by ROAS, deciding to cut "unmeasurable" spend.
- **How to score:** Is each option judged by the right yardstick? Does the plan sacrifice the brand to make the numbers work this month?
- **Quote:** "What you need to sacrifice to make the numbers work might be the very brand you seek to build." (S009)
- **Sources:** S009, S010, S016

## What you charge

### 8. No race to the bottom *(blog)*
- **What it is:** Cutting price is a one-direction, single-axis choice; someone can always go a penny lower. To fund it you cut care, service, and quality. The alternative is the race to the top: be worth it.
- **Parts:** Lowest price is a refuge for those who cannot design something worth paying for. Every great brand is known for something other than cheap. Offer: "You’ll pay more than you hoped but get more than you paid for." (S012)
- **When to use:** White Friday, salary-week sales, coupon codes, matching a competitor's price, "cheapest in Saudi" claims.
- **How to score:** Does the option make the store more worth it, or just cheaper? What gets cut to pay for the discount? An option that competes only on price scores 2/10 or lower on this pillar.
- **Quote:** "Every great brand (even those with low prices) is known for something other than how cheap they are." (S011)
- **Sources:** S011, S012, S013, S014, S017

### 9. Brand as expectations, memories, stories, relationships *(blog)*
- **What it is:** A brand is what makes a buyer choose you over the alternative. Its value is how much extra people pay or how often they choose you. Logo and design only mark that a brand exists.
- **Parts:** Expectations (what they confidently hope for next time), memories, stories, relationships. Hyping expectations for a quick win sets up disappointment (S008).
- **When to use:** Rebrands, new packaging, "premium" claims, over-promising in ads.
- **How to score:** Does the option raise what people will pay or how often they pick us, by keeping a promise? Or does it hype expectations the product cannot keep?
- **Quote:** "A brand’s value is merely the sum total of how much extra people will pay, or how often they choose, the expectations, memories, stories and relationships of one brand over the alternatives." (S007)
- **Sources:** S007, S008, S010
