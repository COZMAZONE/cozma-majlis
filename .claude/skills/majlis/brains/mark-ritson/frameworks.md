# Frameworks: Mark Ritson

9 frameworks, grouped by the job they do (diagnose, choose, advertise, price). "Sources" lists the sources in this brain that teach the framework. No paid course material is used.

---

## Diagnose

### 1. Diagnosis, Strategy, Tactics *(his core teaching model)*
- **What it is:** Marketing in three stages, in order. Diagnosis: understand the market and the customer through research. Strategy: choose where to play and how to win (segmentation, targeting, positioning, objectives). Tactics: only then the 4Ps and communications, channels, and tools.
- **Parts:** "First we build a map of the market from research in the form of a decent segmentation." (S001) Then choose segments, position the brand, and set clear objectives per segment. Then execution.
- **When to use:** Any plan that starts with a channel ("let's do TikTok", "let's run an influencer campaign") or with a discount.
- **How to score:** Can the merchant show the research, the target, the positioning, and the objective behind the option? Missing any step before the tactic: score the option low on this pillar, however clever.
- **Quote:** "Marketing strategy is where we play and how we win in the market. Tactics are how we then deliver on the strategy and execute for success." (S001)
- **Sources:** S001, S004, S014

### 2. Market orientation: you are not the customer *(column; podcast)*
- **What it is:** The first thing a trained marketer learns: your own views are unrepresentative because you are paid to sell the product. Assumptions about customers are conjecture until research tests them. A sample of one (yourself, your friends) is not the market.
- **Parts:** Ignorance of the market leads to assumptions, which lead to bad strategy. Twenty minutes of focus-group data or a basic survey can prevent it (S006).
- **When to use:** Founder intuition calls, "our customers love X", creative chosen because the team likes it.
- **How to score:** Is the option based on customer evidence or team taste? Is there a cheap way to check before spending?
- **Quote:** "What you do and think is highly, highly unrepresentative of your consumer because you’re being paid to market a product, ergo you don’t see that product or service the way the consumers do." (S007)
- **Sources:** S006, S007, S002

## Choose

### 3. Segmentation with data, targeting with courage *(columns; debate)*
- **What it is:** Segment from real data (not trendy attitudes), then choose targets. Targeting is not the opposite of mass reach: you can do sophisticated mass marketing and target specific segments, and sometimes they work best together. Small brands may target because they cannot afford everyone.
- **Parts:** Data-led segmentation; target choice; mass reach where it pays; "A and B", not "A versus B".
- **When to use:** Narrow vs broad audience decisions, multi-segment product lines, a new product for a different buyer.
- **How to score:** Is the target chosen from evidence? Does the plan also build broad salience where the budget allows?
- **Quote:** "We target because we’ve already done sophisticated mass marketing. But you can do both." (S004)
- **Sources:** S002, S004, S003

### 4. Tight positioning: one concept, two or three attributes *(columns 2008 to 2023)*
- **What it is:** Great positioning fits in about three words that are unexpected, born from research, and different from competitors. Long positioning decks and words everyone uses (integrity, quality, excellence, trust, innovation) fail.
- **Parts:** One concept; two or three attributes; one page; held and invested in for years across ads, product, and customer experience.
- **When to use:** Brand positioning, PDP and homepage messaging, competitive positioning, a relaunch.
- **How to score:** Can the merchant state the position in three words that a competitor could not also claim? If the list has more than three, or uses the "usual pointless suspects", score low.
- **Quote:** "Great positioning always consists of unexpected words born from research, forged through heritage and destined to differentiate." (S005)
- **Sources:** S005, S008, S009

### 5. Distinctiveness and differentiation, both *(columns; talks)*
- **What it is:** Distinctiveness (looking like yourself, coming to mind in buying situations) is brilliant and probably more important for overall demand; relative differentiation on a few attributes drives margin and price. You want both.
- **Parts:** Distinctive brand assets used consistently; relative differentiation (more people see you as X than your size predicts) built by saying few things for a long time with real money.
- **When to use:** When Sharp-style "only distinctiveness" arguments meet a pricing or premium decision.
- **How to score:** Does the option build recognizable assets AND a reason to choose and pay more? Only one of them: partial credit.
- **Quote:** "And that’s important, because while distinctiveness and salience are much bigger drivers of overall demand, differentiation is a much bigger driver of margin." (S009)
- **Sources:** S008, S009, S003, S012

## Advertise

### 6. The long and the short (bothism) *(Binet and Field, as he teaches it)*
- **What it is:** Long builds brand equity, salience, and associations; short harnesses and activates them. Do both at campaign level. The long also drives short-term sales, but the short does not build the long. Most brands underspend on the long.
- **Parts:** Split depends on category and brand age (Binet and Field's later work ranges from about 80/20 for financial services to about 50/50 in B2B; young brands need more performance), so treat 60/40 as a mental model, not a rule (S013). Do not mix brand and performance in one ad (S013).
- **When to use:** Brand vs performance budget, ROAS-only reporting, cutting the brand line in a bad month.
- **How to score:** Is there a funded long line and a separate short line? Is the split argued from category and stage, not copied?
- **Quote:** "Don’t think of this as long versus short, the most important word in the long and the short of it is ‘and’," (S012)
- **Sources:** S012, S013, S004

### 7. Effective advertising steps *(Festival of Marketing talk)*
- **What it is:** A practical sequence: write a proper brief with SMART objectives; set budget by zero-based logic tied to returns, not as a percentage of revenue; do long and short; use diverse media; look like yourself (distinctive assets); find a big creative idea; use emotion; pre-test; do not pull ads that are still working.
- **When to use:** Planning any campaign or creative brief.
- **How to score:** Count the steps the option skips. Missing brief, objectives, or pre-test are the costly gaps.
- **Quote:** "If you want to drive up recall, you’ve got to codify it," (S012)
- **Sources:** S012, S013

## Price

### 8. The case against price promotion (ten reasons) *(columns 2023 to 2026)*
- **What it is:** A 20% cut needs roughly double the volume to make the same money; discounts lower the reference price; most promotional volume comes from existing buyers stockpiling; demand falls the next period; promotions become an addiction; loyal full-price buyers feel cheated; retailers and competitors demand and match deals; it signals "we are like everyone else"; and it confirms the rest of the company's doubts about marketing.
- **Parts:** The damage is in the gap between the intended price and the promoted price. Value-added promotions (competitions, added value) can build equity; price cuts rarely do. P&G uses promotion only to drive trial (S011).
- **When to use:** White Friday, salary week, National Day offers, coupon codes, influencer codes, matching a competitor.
- **How to score:** Do the margin math out loud. Is the volume new buyers or pulled-forward existing buyers? Is there a value-added alternative?
- **Quote:** "Cut the price by 20% on a typical consumer margin, and you need to roughly double your volume just to make the same money." (S011)
- **Sources:** S009, S010, S011

### 9. Escaping promotion addiction (seven steps, IGMFUCK) *(column 2023)*
- **What it is:** For a brand already hooked on deals: look at profit and who buys on promotion, not just sales; check that the full price is right with data; win senior management's patience before you start; rebuild differentiation to justify the full price; expect a volume drop before recovery (Tyrrells lost 27% volume after cutting promotions, then recovered with a premium position).
- **When to use:** A store whose sales depend on constant codes and sales.
- **How to score:** Does the plan have a profit dashboard, a verified price, leadership buy-in, and a positioning reason to pay full price?
- **Quote:** "Most organisations develop a promotional addiction because of an over-emphasis on sales and an ignorance of profit." (S009)
- **Sources:** S009
