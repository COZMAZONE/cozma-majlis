# Stories: Chris Do

9 stories and teaching cases from the sources, most useful to a merchant first. Figures are as he tells them in role plays and workshops; several are hypothetical numbers used to teach.

1. **The $18,000 logo and the hourly question.** In a role play a client asks for his hourly rate on an $18,000 logo. He walks the client through the logic: if he works faster the client would pay less, so hourly billing punishes being good; and a great logo delivered fast is worth more, because the client can go to market sooner (S002, S003). Lesson: pay for the result, not the time.

2. **Webcams to brand strategy, $480,000 at stake.** A designer pitching a small job is coached to stop pitching and ask questions. The client's goal turns out to be worth about $480,000, while she planned to spend about $10,000, so he asks whether 2.5% is a serious investment and whether past vendors were underpaid for a self-diagnosed problem (S001). Lesson: the size of the prize sets the budget.

3. **1x, 10x, 100x for the same logo.** He asks a class what they charge for a logo (answers from $500 to $20,000) and explains that the same mark is worth a sandwich to a mom-and-pop shop and a fortune to a company with a $40 million printing budget, because of risk and reach (S002). Lesson: invest in proportion to what the asset touches.

4. **The $1,000 banger video.** A shop owner wants a $1,000 video to bring two or three people a day into the store. He works out that is about $7,500 a month of new revenue, says $1,000 is not enough for that goal, and offers to do it for a share of results per new customer; the owner hesitates (S009). Lesson: when the budget does not fit the goal, change the deal, not the quality.

5. **Startups are code for broke.** Asked who they serve, creatives say "startups". He points to the one place startups have money, raising capital, and a peer who charges up to 5% for pitch decks that raise $10 million (S008). Lesson: find clients with a real, funded problem and sell the money outcome.

6. **Under Armour's logo.** He praises the idea (U, A, and an X) but calls the form ugly, says he almost wants to remove it from clothing he owns, and uses it to teach the aesthetic test, application mockups, and the timeless-brands board (S011). Lesson: a clever idea does not excuse a weak form.

7. **"What is elegant?"** A student asked him to define the word he kept using in critiques, and he realised he had no shared definition; a colleague taught him to list the five things he looks for and let students critique against them (S012). Lesson: turn taste words into criteria.

8. **Four pieces in a FedEx box.** He got his first advertising job by sending only four portfolio pieces chosen to feel like advertising; he tells students to show three strong pieces, because reviewers judge you by your worst one (S007). Lesson: edit ruthlessly.

9. **From Blind to The Futur.** He founded the motion design studio Blind in 1995, won an Emmy, taught at Art Center, and in 2014 started an online school with Jose Caballer that became The Futur, though he was at first uncomfortable on camera (S015). Lesson: he teaches from running a creative business.
