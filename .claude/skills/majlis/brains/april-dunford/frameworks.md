# Frameworks: April Dunford

10 frameworks, grouped by job (define, compete, frame, target, sell). Quotes are verbatim from her blog and interviews. Book structures not confirmed in a fetched source are paraphrased. Each has an e-commerce note for a Saudi store on Salla or Zid.

---

## Define

### 1. Positioning as context *(Obviously Awesome)*
- **What it is:** Positioning sets the context a buyer uses to understand a product, like the opening scene of a movie. It triggers assumptions about competitors, features, target buyer, and price.
- **Parts:** Her definition: positioning defines how your product is a leader at delivering something a well-defined set of customers cares a lot about.
- **When to use:** Before any messaging, PDP rewrite, or ad angle debate.
- **How to score:** Write the assumptions a stranger makes from the current headline and category. Count how many are true. Wrong assumptions (for example "cheaper copy of the pharmacy brand") mean weak positioning.
- **Quote:** "Positioning defines how your product is a leader at delivering something that a well-defined set of customers cares a lot about." (S001)
- **Quote:** "Good positioning sets off a set of assumptions about my product that are true." (S001)
- **Sources:** S001, S006, S011, S012

### 2. The five components, in order *(Obviously Awesome)*
- **What it is:** A method, not a fill-in-the-blank statement. Components depend on each other, so the order matters.
- **Parts:** (1) Competitive alternatives: what would customers do if we didn't exist. (2) Unique attributes: what we have that alternatives do not. (3) Value: the "so what" for customers. (4) Best-fit customers: who cares a lot about that value. (5) Market category: the context where that value is obvious. Optional: relevant trends.
- **When to use:** Repositioning a product, launching a new line, deciding how to compete with a bigger brand.
- **How to score:** Can the team answer each in order, with customer evidence? Skipping step 1 and starting from features or vision scores low.
- **Quote:** "We start with competitive alternatives, or what would customers do if our solution didn't exist." (S001)
- **Quote:** "Our best market category is the context we position our product in such that our value is obvious to our target customers." (S001)
- **Quote:** "I believe this exercise is not only pointless but potentially dangerous." (S001, on the traditional positioning statement)
- **Sources:** S001, S002

## Compete

### 3. Competitive alternatives, not competitors *(Obviously Awesome)*
- **What it is:** The real competition is what the buyer would do instead, starting with the status quo. Only solutions that actually land on the buyer's shortlist count.
- **Parts:** Status quo (doing nothing, a manual workaround, the product already in the cupboard); shortlist competitors; not every company in the category.
- **When to use:** Any "how do we beat X" question; any launch plan that names competitors.
- **How to score:** Has the team listed what customers did before buying, from interviews or reviews? Is the status quo on the list?
- **Quote:** "To win business, the first competitive alternative we need to beat is the status quo." (S004)
- **Quote:** "These status quo solutions are chosen because they are easy to get started with, cheap or free, and frequently do the job good enough in a pinch." (S004)
- **Quote:** "The more deeply you understand your prospects' status quo and which other solutions land on a shortlist with you, the stronger your positioning will be." (S004)
- **E-commerce note:** For a collagen store the alternatives are: the pharmacy's collagen, a clinic treatment, another online brand on Noon or Amazon, food and home remedies, or doing nothing and living with it. The pharmacy wins on trust and convenience; know exactly where you beat it.
- **Sources:** S004, S010

### 4. Unique attributes to value ("so what?") *(Obviously Awesome)*
- **What it is:** Differentiation only counts relative to the alternatives, and an attribute matters only when translated into value for the customer, ideally with proof.
- **When to use:** PDP feature lists, ingredient-heavy copy, claims every competitor also makes.
- **How to score:** For each claimed attribute: do the alternatives have it too? If yes, drop it from the lead. If no, what does it let her do or feel, and what proves it?
- **Quote:** "Great positioning is a process of deeply understanding what sets you apart from the other solutions in the market, the value that differentiator brings to customers, and which customers care the most about that value." (S006)
- **Sources:** S001, S006, S002

## Frame

### 5. Market category as frame of reference *(Obviously Awesome)*
- **What it is:** The category you choose decides who you are compared to and on what criteria. A winner in one frame is a loser in another.
- **Parts:** She describes styles of framing; "use an existing frame and dominate the category" fits market leaders (S005). The book's other styles, paraphrased: win a subsegment of an existing category (big fish, small pond), or create a new category (costly, needs education).
- **When to use:** When the product is being judged against the wrong rival, or customers ask "how are you better than [giant]?"
- **How to score:** In the current frame, are we the obvious leader for someone? If not, is there a subsegment frame where we are?
- **Quote:** "A winner in one category is a loser in another" (S003)
- **Quote:** "Startups will often place their products within a frame that fails to highlight their strengths and often puts their competitors at an advantage." (S005)
- **Quote:** "You are unlikely to out-Coke Coke but you might still be able to be the best virtual reality headset on the market if you hurry." (S005)
- **E-commerce note:** "Collagen supplement" puts you next to the pharmacy shelf and its price. A tighter frame (for example a routine for a specific need and buyer, sold with a guided program) can make the same product the obvious choice for that segment. Confirm any new frame against the store's approved claim language.
- **Sources:** S001, S003, S005, S006, S015

### 6. Don't let customers decide what you are
- **What it is:** If you do not position deliberately, the market or the customer positions you, often in a category that makes your strengths irrelevant.
- **Quote:** "Customers will decide what you are, using the clues they can most easily assemble." (S003)
- **Sources:** S003, S002

## Target

### 7. Best-fit customer segmentation *(Obviously Awesome)*
- **What it is:** Target the customers who care most about your differentiated value, described by the characteristics that make them care, then go broader later.
- **Quote:** "What are the characteristics of prospects that love my unique stuff the most?" (S007)
- **Quote:** "In my experiences the smaller and tighter you can get on your segmentation, the easier it is to get early traction. You can always go broader later." (S007)
- **E-commerce note:** Mine reviews and repeat buyers: who reorders, what they had tried before, what they say changed. Aim ads and LPs at that segment, not "women 25 to 45".
- **Sources:** S007, S001

### 8. New vs established markets
- **What it is:** In an established market buyers know the problem and shop for solutions; in a new market they do not know they have the problem, so the first job is education.
- **Quote:** "In new markets, prospects are blissfully unaware that they even have a problem." (S008)
- **Quote:** "For new markets the first job of marketing is to educate your target segment that there is a problem to be solved in the first place." (S008)
- **Sources:** S008

## Sell

### 9. Positioning before storytelling
- **What it is:** Story frameworks (she names Building a StoryBrand as one she likes) assume you already know your target customer, differentiated value, and competitors. Without positioning, the story is built on guesses.
- **Quote:** "But starting with storytelling without working on positioning first is dangerous." (S009)
- **Sources:** S009, S010

### 10. Sales Pitch: insight-led, guide the buyer *(Sales Pitch)*
- **What it is:** Buyers are overwhelmed and afraid of a bad choice, so many purchases end in no decision. The pitch should teach the market and help them choose, starting from a point of view, not a generic problem or a feature tour.
- **Parts (paraphrased from S013):** open with your insight about the market; describe the alternatives and where each falls short; describe what a great solution looks like; introduce the product; show differentiated value with proof; handle objections; ask for the next step. She calls it an eight-section structure.
- **When to use:** LPs, long PDPs, video sales letters, WhatsApp sales conversations, launch pages.
- **How to score:** Does the page help her choose between the real alternatives, or only list our features as if all were equal?
- **Quote:** "What customers actually want from a sales rep in a sales situation is they want perspectives on the market." (S013)
- **Quote:** "If we're showing everything like all these features are equal, we're not really homing in on what makes us different and special." (S013)
- **Quote:** "And so we as vendors, I think need to adopt the attitude of we're really here to guide the customer." (S013)
- **Sources:** S013, S010
