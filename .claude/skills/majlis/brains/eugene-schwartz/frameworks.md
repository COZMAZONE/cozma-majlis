# Frameworks: Eugene Schwartz

24 frameworks, grouped by the job they do (find the desire, place the market, write the hook, build conviction in the body, work habits). "Sources" counts distinct sources in the corpus that teach or apply the framework. Book tags point to the chapter of *Breakthrough Advertising* (1966); the book's chapter ids are S001 to S016. Scoring scales are this seat's operational translation for judging a creative, not his own numbers.

---

## Find the desire

### 1. Mass Desire: channel, never create *(BA ch. 1)*
- **What it is:** The power of an ad comes from a desire that already exists in a large number of people. Copy cannot create it or fight it; it can only focus it onto one product. Trying to create desire turns advertising into education, which pays back at best one for one.
- **Parts:** Mass desire is "the public spread of a private want" (S002). Three dimensions to score each candidate desire: urgency (intensity of demand), staying power (it does not get satisfied), scope (how many people share it). Pick the one strongest desire; only one can lead the headline.
- **When to use:** Before any hook or concept. When a team is "educating the market" about something nobody asked for.
- **How to score:** Rate the desire the creative leads with 1 to 10 on urgency, staying power, and scope; average them. Under 6 means the ad is pushing a want the market does not feel. Deduct 3 if the creative argues against an existing desire instead of riding it.
- **Quote:** "When it tries to create this desire, it is no longer advertising but education." (S002)
- **Sources:** 7 (S002, S007, S008, S013, S019, S028, S037)

### 2. Permanent forces and forces of change *(BA ch. 1)*
- **What it is:** An inventory of where desires come from. Permanent forces: mass instincts (to look attractive, to stay healthy) and unsolved mass problems. Forces of change: style shifts and mass education (what celebrities, media, and neighbours make normal).
- **Parts:** For a permanent force the job is a fresh appeal and stronger believability against rivals; for a force of change the job is timing, catching the tide early and shifting appeals as it turns. Detecting these is a daily lifelong study.
- **When to use:** Choosing campaign themes, seasonal pivots, deciding whether a trend (a new ingredient, a TikTok routine) is big enough to ride.
- **How to score:** Name the force behind the creative. Permanent force with a fresh angle: 7 to 9. Trend caught early with evidence of spread: 8 to 10. Trend already exhausted or invented by the brand: 2 to 4.
- **Quote:** "This is a study that will occupy part of every working day for the rest of his life." (S002)
- **Sources:** 4 (S002, S006, S017, S019)

### 3. The functional product and the one dominant performance *(BA ch. 1)*
- **What it is:** Every product is two products: the physical product (what it is) and the functional product (what it does). Only what it does sells; physical facts are proof, price justification, and mechanism, never the headline.
- **Parts:** (1) List every performance the product delivers. (2) Group each against the desire it satisfies. (3) Feature the one with the most power right now. (4) Use the rest, and the physical facts, to reinforce it.
- **When to use:** Product pages and ads that lead with ingredients, milligrams, or packaging.
- **How to score:** Does the hook state a performance (what she gets) rather than a spec? Yes and single-minded: 8 to 10. Leads with a spec or with three benefits at once: 3 to 5.
- **Quote:** "Every product gives you dozens of keys. But only one will fit the lock." (S002)
- **Sources:** 5 (S002, S006, S009, S019, S038)

## Place the market

### 4. Five stages of awareness *(BA ch. 2)*
- **What it is:** Markets sit at different distances from your product, and each stage needs a different headline. Each stage is "separated from the others by a psychological wall" (S003).
- **Parts:** (1) Most aware: knows and wants the product; state name and price or offer. (2) Product aware: knows it but is not convinced; reinforce, sharpen, extend, prove, or add a new mechanism. (3) Solution aware (wants the result, does not know a product exists): name the desire, prove it can be done, show the mechanism is in this product. (4) Problem aware (feels a need, no link to a solution): name and dramatize the need, then present the product as the inevitable answer. (5) Unaware: no price, no product name, no direct claim; an identification headline that calls the market together. Modern labels follow S041; the book's own wording is in S003.
- **When to use:** Every hook review, every audience/placement pairing (retargeting is near stage 1 or 2; cold TikTok is often 4 or 5).
- **How to score:** Name the audience's stage, then the stage the hook is written for. Match: 8 to 10. One stage off: 5. Two or more off (a price-led hook to cold viewers, a long problem story to cart abandoners): 1 to 3.
- **Quote:** "The more aware your market, the easier the selling job, the less you need to say." (S003)
- **Sources:** 8 (S002, S003, S007, S010, S012, S019, S034, S041)

### 5. Five stages of market sophistication *(BA ch. 3)*
- **What it is:** How many similar claims the market has already heard. Answered by a few hours of competitor research.
- **Parts:** (1) First in market: simple, direct claim, "don't be fancy". (2) Second: copy the working claim but enlarge it, drive it to the limit. (3) Claims exhausted: introduce a new mechanism, headline leads with how it works. (4) Rivals copy the mechanism: enlarge the mechanism (easier, faster, surer). (5) Exhausted market: shift to identification with the prospect himself.
- **When to use:** Any crowded category (collagen, whitening, hair growth). When ads all say the same benefit and CPMs buy only skepticism.
- **How to score:** Collect 10 competitor ads. If the hook repeats their claim in their words: 2 to 4. Same claim with a credible, specific new mechanism: 7 to 9. Identification hook in a stage-5 market: 8 to 10. Bigger-number claim in a stage 3+ market: 2 (and a compliance risk).
- **Quote:** "Believability is shattered; claims are automatically discounted 50% by their readers." (S004)
- **Sources:** 6 (S003, S004, S005, S012, S034, S041)

### 6. The three questions before any headline *(BA ch. 2, ch. 3)*
- **What it is:** The headline's content comes from three answers: the mass desire behind the market, the market's state of awareness, and its state of sophistication.
- **Parts:** Desire gives the force; awareness and sophistication give the location; the strategy for meeting both gives the words.
- **When to use:** As the opening checklist of any creative brief.
- **How to score:** A brief that answers all three in one line each: 9. Missing sophistication (no competitor scan): cap at 5.
- **Quote:** "This third question is the most easily answered." (S004)
- **Sources:** 3 (S003, S004, S006)

## Write the hook

### 7. The headline's real job (sentence-to-sentence pull) *(BA ch. 2)*
- **What it is:** A headline does not need to sell, name the product, or even state the main appeal. Its job is to stop the right prospect and make him read the next sentence; each sentence has the same job.
- **Parts:** Stop. Select (pick the logical prospects and reject the rest). Pull into line two. Only the fully aware, offered a special price, can be sold by the headline alone.
- **When to use:** First 1 to 3 seconds of a video, the first line of a caption, the hero line of a landing page.
- **How to score:** Would the target viewer watch second 4? Does the hook exclude the wrong people? Strong stop plus strong selection: 9. Clickbait that stops everyone and selects no one: 3.
- **Quote:** "Actually, your headline does not need to sell at all." (S003)
- **Sources:** 5 (S002, S003, S006, S007, S016)

### 8. The identification headline for unaware or exhausted markets *(BA ch. 2, ch. 3)*
- **What it is:** When direct claims fail, call the market together by echoing an emotion, attitude, or private dissatisfaction, so the reader recognises herself. Patterns: give words to a hidden dream, exploit a hidden fear, start from a universally accepted image, project a hidden desire that cannot be said bluntly, use a common resentment, project the result of a problem, project an ultimate triumph.
- **Parts:** Eliminate price, product name, and direct claim from the headline; speak only to the market's state of mind; bring the product in later.
- **When to use:** Cold audiences who do not believe their problem is solvable, or a category saturated with claims.
- **How to score:** Read the hook to a target customer: does she say "that is me"? 8 to 10. A clever or funny line with no market in it: 2. His rule: an empty headline wastes the client's money.
- **Quote:** "Your prospect must identify with your headline before he can buy from it." (S003)
- **Sources:** 4 (S003, S004, S009, S017)

### 9. Verbalization: 38 ways to strengthen a headline *(BA ch. 4)*
- **What it is:** Once the content is right, raise its impact by how it is said: measure size or speed, compare, metaphorize, sensitize (taste, touch, sight), demonstrate, dramatize, paradox, name the problem, warn, ask and answer, challenge a limiting belief, and so on.
- **Parts:** Three goals: strengthen the claim, make it fresh again, pull the reader into the body. Variations are bound into the main claim, not added beside it.
- **When to use:** After the desire, stage, and mechanism are agreed; polishing three hook variants for a test.
- **How to score:** Count how many devices sharpen the single claim without changing it; one or two well used: 8. Devices piled on until the claim blurs: 4. Bare claim in a first-stage market: fine, score on fit.
- **Quote:** "And it is the art of increasing the impact of a headline by the way in which it is stated." (S005)
- **Sources:** 3 (S005, S016, S039)

### 10. Three levels of creativity and the echo ad *(BA ch. 5)*
- **What it is:** Word substitution (swap your product into someone else's headline), formula filling, and the analytical approach. Only the third works for a unique product, market, and timing; the first produces "Echo Ads" that remind people of someone else.
- **Parts:** Analyse the problem into parts, find the one unique element in product and market, then build it up. A solution can be used only once.
- **When to use:** When a team "adapts" a competitor's or a viral ad, or reuses its own old winners.
- **How to score:** Could this hook run for a rival brand with the logo changed? Yes: 2 to 3. Built from something only this product and this market have: 8 to 10.
- **Quote:** "They are the kind of ads that clients write, rather than copy writers." (S006)
- **Sources:** 3 (S006, S015, S032)

## Build conviction in the body

### 11. Desires, Identifications, Beliefs (the three dimensions) *(BA ch. 6)*
- **What it is:** Body copy changes the prospect's picture of reality along three dimensions: what she wants, who she wants to be, and what she already believes. Copy length is set by how much work each dimension needs.
- **Parts:** Desire: expand, sharpen, channel, give a goal. Identification: put the role she wants behind the product. Belief: build from her existing beliefs, in her logic, never argue with them.
- **When to use:** Deciding copy length, product page structure, video beats after the hook.
- **How to score:** Score each dimension 1 to 10 for the asset; the lowest number is the next fix.
- **Quote:** "But you can expand them, sharpen them, channel them, and give them a goal." (S007)
- **Sources:** 5 (S007, S008, S009, S010, S037)

### 12. Intensification *(BA ch. 7)*
- **What it is:** Making desire vivid with concrete scenes of fulfillment. Thirteen ways include: first presentation of claims, claims in action, bring in the reader, show how to test the claims, stretch benefits over time, bring in an audience (testimonials), experts approving, compare and prove superiority, picture the black side, show how easy it is, metaphor, summarize, put the guarantee to work.
- **Parts:** One dominant idea, presented from as many fresh angles as possible without boredom. Short media use compression plus campaign repetition.
- **When to use:** Video body, product page benefits, UGC selection (each clip a new angle on the same desire).
- **How to score:** Count distinct, concrete scenes of the one benefit (not new benefits). 4 or more fresh angles on one desire: 8 to 10. Abstract adjectives ("premium", "luxury"): 2 to 4.
- **Quote:** "You are literally the script writer for your prospect's dreams." (S008)
- **Sources:** 4 (S008, S012, S017, S022)

### 13. Identification and the bridge image *(BA ch. 8)*
- **What it is:** Every product should offer two reasons to buy: the physical satisfaction and the role it lets the buyer play (character roles such as elegant or disciplined, achievement roles such as successful). Images must be ones the market can believe of itself; use a bridge image from her side to reach an aspirational one.
- **Parts:** Product as instrument for achieving the role; product as acknowledgement the role is achieved; limits of believable images; identifications drawn from the physical product (look, structure, background).
- **When to use:** Casting, styling, premium positioning, choosing between real customers and polished models.
- **How to score:** Would the target customer believe this person could be her? Real, close-to-her people with one aspirational cue: 8 to 10. Elite or unreachable imagery for a mass market: 2 to 4.
- **Quote:** "Many campaigns have collapsed because they have asked their market to identify themselves with an unbelievable image." (S009)
- **Sources:** 4 (S007, S009, S010, S037)

### 14. Gradualization (the architecture of belief) *(BA ch. 9)*
- **What it is:** Start with a statement the reader fully accepts and build a chain of acceptances to a goal conclusion she would have rejected if stated first. Devices: the inclusion question, detailed identification, contradiction of present false beliefs, the language of logic, syllogistic steps.
- **Parts:** Every claim draws strength from its content and from its preparation. Never violate an established belief; route around it.
- **When to use:** Skeptical categories (supplements), long-form landing pages, objection-heavy products.
- **How to score:** Is the first claim something she already agrees with? Does each step earn the next? Strong chain: 8 to 10. Big promise in line one to a skeptic: 3.
- **Quote:** "Belief ultimately depends upon structure." (S010)
- **Sources:** 3 (S010, S011, S037)

### 15. Redefinition *(BA ch. 10)*
- **What it is:** Give the product a new definition to remove a built-in objection before the prospect notices it. The classic is concept judo: turn a liability into an asset.
- **Parts:** Simplification (too hard becomes easy), escalation (not important enough becomes important everywhere), price reduction (too expensive becomes cheap by switching the comparison to a more expensive standard).
- **When to use:** Price objections, "too much effort" products, products that seem trivial.
- **How to score:** Is the main objection named and reframed, with the reframe true? 8 to 10. Objection ignored: 4. Reframe that misleads (fake "was" price): 1 and a red line.
- **Quote:** "Redefinition is the process of giving a new definition to your product." (S011)
- **Sources:** 3 (S011, S012, S034)

### 16. Mechanization *(BA ch. 11)*
- **What it is:** Verbal proof of how the product works, answering the reader's silent "How does it work?" Stages: name the mechanism (known), describe it (unknown), feature it (when rivals share the promise). Price cuts also need a mechanism, a reason why.
- **Parts:** Promise, then reason why, at the exact point the reader starts to doubt. Write as copywriter and prospect at once.
- **When to use:** Sophistication stage 3+, any discount or offer, ingredient stories.
- **How to score:** Can a viewer explain in one sentence why this works, and is the explanation true and allowed? 8 to 10. A buzzword mechanism (patented complex, secret formula) with no substance: 3.
- **Quote:** "To write good copy, you have to play a dual role." (S012)
- **Sources:** 5 (S004, S012, S013, S038, S035)

### 17. Concentration *(BA ch. 12)*
- **What it is:** A documented direct comparison that proves other ways of satisfying the desire fall short, always paired with how this product fixes that weakness. Used when you are the challenger and the prospects are a rival's customers.
- **Parts:** Five competitive weapons: superior product, superior promise, product role, response and escalation, direct attack. Never attack without supplying the solution.
- **When to use:** Comparison sections, "why not just use X" objections.
- **How to score:** Is the weakness real, documented, and answered by this product, without naming or smearing a rival unfairly? 8 to 10. Pure attack: 2.
- **Quote:** "Never attack a weakness unless you can provide the solution to that weakness at the same time!" (S013)
- **Sources:** 2 (S013, S015)

### 18. Camouflage *(BA ch. 13)*
- **What it is:** Borrowing the believability stored in a medium by matching its format, style, and phraseology, so the reader shifts from editorial to ad with the least friction.
- **Parts:** Format, editorial style, phraseology of the publication. A single format change can lift response sharply.
- **When to use:** Platform-native creative (looks like a normal post, a creator's own video). Today: native style yes, disguise no.
- **How to score:** Native to the placement and clearly an ad: 8 to 10. Fake news layout, fake review, or fake chat screenshot: 0 and a red line.
- **Quote:** "A single change in format can add 50% to your readership, and your results." (S014)
- **Sources:** 2 (S014, S003)

### 19. Reinforcement and Interweaving *(BA ch. 14)*
- **What it is:** Join two emotional images so their combined impact exceeds the sum (Avis: "we're second" plus "we try harder"); weave emotion, image, and logic into the same sentence so every line does two jobs.
- **Parts:** Juxtaposition (frank admission next to a strong claim), each element serving desire and belief at once; techniques become intuitive with practice.
- **When to use:** Tightening scripts and product copy; turning honest limits into credibility.
- **How to score:** Count lines doing two jobs (sell plus prove, image plus logic). Most lines: 8 to 10. Separate blocks of hype then specs: 4.
- **Quote:** "In emotional writing, one plus one can often equal ten." (S015)
- **Sources:** 2 (S015, S016)

### 20. Momentum and Mood *(BA ch. 14)*
- **What it is:** Momentum keeps the reader going one more sentence, raising questions she must read on to answer while still selling. Mood is set by word choice and rhythm, unnoticed by the reader.
- **Parts:** Open loops, numbered actions, cascading promises; rhythm matched to the feeling (excitement, sincerity, calm).
- **When to use:** Video pacing and retention, long product pages, email.
- **How to score:** Retention curve or read-depth if available; otherwise, does each beat create a reason to see the next? 8 to 10 with no dead beats.
- **Quote:** "The objective here is to keep the reader going" (S016)
- **Sources:** 2 (S016, S015)

## Work habits

### 21. Copy assembly from research
- **What it is:** He said he did not write copy, he assembled it: read the source (often a client's book) several times, highlight every claim, have the lines typed up, then arrange them into headline, subheads, sections, and bullets.
- **Parts:** Know the product better than its maker; the source holds the answers, the copywriter finds the questions (fascinations); create only after assembly.
- **When to use:** Every new product page or ad: start from the brand's own product documents, reviews, and customer words.
- **How to score:** Can each claim in the creative be traced to a source document or real customer quote? All: 9 to 10. Invented copy: 3.
- **Quote:** "I want to know the book as well as or even BETTER than the author!" (S020)
- **Sources:** 9 (S020, S025, S027, S029, S030, S033, S035, S036, S038)

### 22. The 33:33 timer
- **What it is:** Work in blocks of 33 minutes 33 seconds; during a block he could do anything related to the copy, including nothing, but not leave the chair. Then a short break. About three hours a day, five days a week.
- **Parts:** No goal for the block, no willpower, just presence; boredom pushes you into the work; stop when the timer rings.
- **When to use:** Producing hook batches, scripts, page copy without writer's block.
- **How to score:** Process framework, not a creative score; use it to plan production sprints.
- **Quote:** "It is the most valuable thing I ever bought in my life." (S017)
- **Sources:** 6 (S017, S018, S024, S035, S036, S039)

### 23. The listening diet (market research as a daily habit)
- **What it is:** Stay in touch with ordinary people: read mass-market media and junk, watch what the crowd watches, talk to taxi drivers, listen far more than you talk. The market's own words are the copy.
- **Parts:** Two hours a week minimum on where the market is; an hour a day of reading outside your business; listen to the person with the problem, her social layer, and the society.
- **When to use:** Before briefs; mining TikTok comments, WhatsApp replies, reviews, and customer service chats in the customers' own dialect.
- **How to score:** Does the creative use phrases real customers use? Verbatim customer language in the hook: 8 to 10. Brand-speak: 3.
- **Quote:** "Talk little, listen much. That is your market talking." (S017)
- **Sources:** 6 (S006, S017, S018, S019, S029, S034)

### 24. Swipe file and fascinations
- **What it is:** He kept and studied past ads, and with Boardroom-era writers he is credited with fascinations (curiosity bullets), often with page numbers pointing to the book's answer.
- **Parts:** Study winners for strategy, not to word-swap (see #10); write bullets that promise a specific, intriguing answer the product delivers.
- **When to use:** Product page bullets, carousel cards, email subject lines.
- **How to score:** Each bullet specific, curiosity-raising, and truly answered by the product: 8 to 10. Vague or unanswerable teasers: 3.
- **Quote:** "Gene Schwartz was a swipe file fiend himself" (S032)
- **Sources:** 4 (S030, S032, S033, S040)
