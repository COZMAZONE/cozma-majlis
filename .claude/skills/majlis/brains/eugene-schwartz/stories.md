# Stories: Eugene Schwartz

16 stories and case studies he used or that are told about him, most-useful first. Figures are as the sources tell them.

1. **The Edsel and the cars that fought the tide.** Chrysler sold a roomy, boxy car when the public wanted longer, lower, wider; Ford sold safety when buyers wanted horsepower; the Edsel died fighting the swing to compacts. Lesson: no budget beats mass desire. (S002)

2. **Five stages of the cigarette market.** From "I'd walk a mile for a Camel" (simple claim) to bigger promises, to mechanisms ("toasted", "T-zone"), to enlarged mechanisms, to pure identification (Marlboro men); filters then replayed all five stages. Lesson: every crowded category walks the same ladder. (S004)

3. **Reducing ads and claim inflation.** "Lose ugly fat" worked when first; then rivals pushed numbers to the limit, readers began discounting claims by half, and winners switched to mechanisms ("floats fat right out of your body"). Lesson: when the market stops believing the number, sell the how; and claim inflation ends in skepticism and regulators. (S004)

4. **"Blow some my way."** A 1920s cigarette ad that could not show a woman smoking opened the female market with four words from a woman to a man on a moonlit beach. Lesson: an identification headline can open a market a direct claim never could. (S004)

5. **"Often a bridesmaid but never a bride" (Listerine).** Bad breath was too insulting to say directly, so the ad projected the result of the problem in a story. Lesson: in an unaware market, lead with the consequence she will identify with. (S003)

6. **Lifebuoy and B.O.** A soap with a harsh medicinal smell redefined the smell as the proof of odor-killing power. Lesson: redefinition turns the product's liability into its asset. (S011)

7. **The TV repair book that failed, then won.** "Do your own TV repairs" scared readers; rebuilt with a chain of acceptances (your set is reliable, small fixes are simple, so stop paying for knowledge), it sold. Lesson: gradualize belief; do not open with the hard part. (S010, S011)

8. **The Wall Street Journal ad.** The same book ad, reset in the Journal's own headline type and old-fashioned subheads, ran nineteen times with no drop in pull. Lesson: native format borrows the medium's trust. (S014)

9. **Rodale letters and the doctor who crossed out "eggs".** He wrote about 30 letters for Rodale that sold millions of books; when an author-doctor struck a claim about eggs, Schwartz quoted page 164 of the doctor's own book back to him. Lesson: know the source better than its author. (S020)

10. **The 33:33 timer.** He carried a timer everywhere, set 33 minutes 33 seconds, and could only sit with the copy or do nothing; boredom pulled him into the work. He told Kurtz simply that it was what worked for him. Lesson: process beats inspiration. (S017, S024, S035, S036)

11. **Copy for names: the Boardroom, Rodale, Instant Improvement alliance.** In the 1980s he wrote landmark packages for Boardroom and Rodale for no fee, taking about 750,000 names from their lists to mail for his own health books instead. Lesson: trade your strength for the asset you lack; the list is the business. (S024, S026)

12. **"I didn't write it. My client wrote it."** The first Boardroom Reports promotion (1972) came out of his interview with founder Marty Edelston; praised for it, he credited the client. Lesson: copy is assembled from the source's own words. (S033)

13. **Eugene Stevens Inc. and fierce 1950s headlines.** From messenger to copy chief at Huber Hoge, then his own mail-order firm (1954) selling memory courses, gas savers, and a wire nail "that cuts through armor plate", at about $4 million a year. Lesson: mail order is the school of copy, and the market forgives nothing. (S023, S001)

14. **National Enquirer and taxi drivers.** A Manhattan art collector who read the lowest tabloids and Weekly World News and talked to every cab driver, because they show what people want and how much they will believe. Lesson: never lose touch with the mass market you sell to. (S017, S018)

15. **The out-of-print bible.** *Breakthrough Advertising* went out of print, copies sold for hundreds to $950 on eBay, Boardroom reprinted small batches to give away, and Kurtz republished it in 2016 unchanged. Lesson: principles about human behavior do not date. (S026, S034, S040)

16. **The Phillips Publishing all-day seminar.** He lectured Phillips' marketing team for a full day; a junior marketer nearly left early and later called it an amazing lecture; the 1993 lecture circulates as "The Lost Secrets". Lesson: his talks, not only the book, carry the habits (listening, timer, research). (S019, S034)
